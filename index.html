📋 Lesson Overview

🎯 The Big Idea

Students apply the editorial judgment skills developed in Lessons 3.4 and 3.5 to their ongoing creative writing projects. This is a working session where students use AI as a creative partner, practicing the prompt engineering techniques from Chapter 3 while developing their stories. The focus is on iterative refinement—using the "editorial eye" to evaluate AI-generated content and guide successive prompts toward better results.

Key Conceptual Goals

By the end of this lesson, students should:

  • Apply systematic evaluation criteria to their own creative work
  • Practice iterative prompting with their creative projects
  • Use context decisions strategically to improve AI responses
  • Exercise editorial judgment to select and refine AI-generated content
  • Reflect on the experience of creative collaboration with AI
  • Document their process and insights in field journals

This Lesson in Context

✅ What Students Already Know:

  • Four basic prompt structures and iteration cycles (Lesson 3.2)
  • How context decisions affect AI responses (Lesson 3.3)
  • Why writing skills enable editorial judgment (Lesson 3.4)

🔮 What This Lesson Prepares Students For:

  • Tomorrow (Lesson 3.7): Discussion Day reflecting on quality and context—students will share experiences from today's creative work
  • Lesson 3.8: Building simulations requiring strong editorial judgment
  • Ongoing Creative Project: Today's work advances their story development
  • Chapter 4: Recognizing bias in AI output builds on editorial judgment skills

👨‍🏫 Detailed Facilitation Guide

Opening: Framing the Creative Session (5-7 minutes)

SUGGESTED OPENING SCRIPT

"Welcome back. Over the past few days, you've been building crucial skills for working with AI:

  • Lesson 3.4: You discovered why writing skills matter—they give you the editorial judgment to recognize quality and give effective direction.
  • Lesson 3.5: You systematically developed your editorial eye, learning to evaluate AI output using specific criteria like clarity, engagement, accuracy, completeness, and audience fit.

"Today, you apply those skills to YOUR creative project. This is a working session—most of our time will be independent work where you're using AI to develop your story. You'll be prompting for story elements, evaluating what AI generates, and making editorial decisions about what to keep, what to revise, and what to discard.

"This is where prompt engineering becomes practical. You're not just learning about iteration—you're actually doing it. You're not just talking about editorial judgment—you're exercising it with every decision."

Key Reminders (2-3 minutes)

Quick reference to best practices:

From Lesson 3.2 - Iteration:

  • "Start with a clear prompt, get a response, evaluate it in 30 seconds, then iterate"
  • "You don't need perfection on the first try—that's what iteration is for"

From Lesson 3.3 - Context:

  • "Think strategically about what context helps vs. what clutters"
  • "Your expertise in your own story is your advantage—use it"

From Lesson 3.5 - Editorial Eye:

  • "Use specific criteria: Is this clear? Engaging? Accurate to my vision? Complete enough? Right for my story?"
  • "If something feels 'off,' identify WHAT feels off so you can prompt for improvement"
SETTING EXPECTATIONS

"For the next 35-40 minutes, you'll work independently on your creative projects. I'll be circulating quietly if you need help, but I won't interrupt your flow.

"Here's what I want you to notice while you work: What does this experience feel like? Are you pulled into the creative process? Do you lose track of time? When do you feel most engaged? When does it feel like work vs. when does it feel like play?

"We'll end with 5-8 minutes for field journal reflection about your experience. But for now—this is your time to create."

Transition to Work Time (30 seconds)

"Open your creative project. Take a deep breath. Let's create."

[Start timer. Consider playing quiet instrumental music if that helps your students focus.]

Independent Creative Work (35-40 minutes)

Student Activities During This Time:

  • Prompting AI for story elements (characters, dialogue, settings, plot points)
  • Reading and evaluating AI responses
  • Iterating to improve output quality
  • Making editorial decisions about what to include in their stories
  • Documenting especially successful prompts or interesting discoveries
  • Advancing their creative projects

👨‍🏫 Your Role During Work Time:

Primary Goal: Protect the working time and allow students to enter flow state.

Circulate Strategically:

  • Move quietly around the room
  • Pause briefly to observe what students are doing
  • Look for evidence of skills application (are they iterating? evaluating? making decisions?)
  • Only interrupt if a student seems truly stuck or off-task

What to Look For:

Observable Behavior What It Tells You
Multiple tabs/windows open, switching between AI and project Student is iterating and integrating AI output
Long pauses while reading AI responses Student is evaluating carefully (good!)
Rapid iteration (prompt → evaluate → prompt again quickly) Editorial eye is functioning—they know what they want
Writing in field journal during work time Documenting insights as they occur
Scrolling back through prior responses Making comparisons, building on earlier attempts
Frustrated body language but still working Normal creative struggle—monitor but don't interrupt
Staring at blank screen for 5+ minutes May need gentle check-in

Supporting Students Without Disrupting Flow

If a student raises their hand or clearly needs help, approach quietly and ask in a low voice: "What are you working on?" or "Where are you stuck?"

Common issues and quick responses:

  • "I don't know what to prompt for"
    → "What part of your story needs development right now?"
    → "Look at your project outline—what's the next element you need?"
  • "The AI isn't giving me what I want"
    → "Show me what you prompted and what you got. What's missing?"
    → "Use your evaluation criteria from Lesson 3.5—what specifically is off?"
  • "I can't decide which response is better"
    → "Use your rubric from yesterday. Which is clearer? More engaging? More accurate to your vision?"
  • "This response is pretty good but not quite right"
    → "That's perfect for iteration. What specific change would make it right? Tell AI exactly that."

⚠️ Avoid Over-Helping

The temptation during Project Days is to "teach" or "correct" too much. Resist this. Students learn by doing and sometimes by struggling productively.

Don't: Hover over students, offer unsolicited advice, interrupt students who are clearly engaged, make editorial decisions for them

Do: Trust the process, let productive struggle happen, respond to requests for help, note patterns to address in tomorrow's discussion

Time Checks

  • At 20 minutes remaining: Quiet verbal reminder: "About 20 minutes of work time left."
  • At 10 minutes remaining: "Ten minutes left for creative work. Start finding a good stopping point."
  • At 5 minutes remaining: "Last 5 minutes. Finish your current thought and prepare to transition to reflection."

Field Journal Reflection (5-8 minutes)

TRANSITION TO REFLECTION

"Alright, let's pause the creative work. Save where you are. For the next 5-8 minutes, I want you to reflect on the experience you just had.

"This isn't about evaluating your story quality—that's for later. This is about noticing what the experience of working with AI felt like.

"Open your field journals."

Field Journal Prompts

Display these questions on the board or screen:

Field Journal Reflection Prompts:

1. Flow and Engagement:

  • To what degree were you pulled into the creative process? Did you lose track of time?
  • When did you feel most engaged? When did it feel like work vs. play?

2. Editorial Judgment in Action:

  • Give one example of using your editorial eye today. What did you evaluate and what decision did you make?

3. The Partnership Experience:

  • What did working with AI feel like? More like using a tool? Having a conversation? Something else?
  • When was AI most helpful? When was it least helpful?

4. Process Insights:

  • What prompt engineering technique worked well today? (iteration, context decisions, specific evaluation criteria)
  • What would you do differently next time?

Write for 5-8 minutes. Be specific. Use examples from your work today.

Why These Questions Matter:

  • Flow and Engagement: Helps students notice when they're in optimal creative states, builds metacognitive awareness
  • Editorial Judgment: Reinforces the skills from Lesson 3.5, makes the connection between practice and application explicit
  • Partnership Experience: Surfaces feelings about human-AI collaboration, prepares for deeper discussions about creativity
  • Process Insights: Identifies what's working for individual students, creates documentation for future reference

During Reflection Time: Students should write in silence. Some will write a lot, others less—both are fine. Goal is thoughtful consideration, not length.

At 3 minutes remaining: "About 3 minutes left. Make sure you address at least 2-3 of the prompts."

Brief Closing (2 minutes)

CLOSING SCRIPT

"Thank you for your focused work today. You applied real prompt engineering skills to real creative challenges—exactly what this chapter is about.

"Tomorrow in Lesson 3.7, we'll have a discussion about what you learned today and back in Lesson 3.5. We'll connect your quality evaluation experiences to your context decisions from Lesson 3.3. We'll also talk more about that 'back of the mind voice'—the editorial instinct that guides your work.

"Your field journal entries from today will be the foundation for tomorrow's discussion, so what you wrote matters.

"Any quick questions before we end?"

Quick Checkout Options (if time allows):

  • "Thumbs up if you made good progress today, thumbs sideways if it was challenging, thumbs down if you're frustrated."
  • "One word that describes your creative session today—just call it out."
  • "On a scale of 1-5, how engaged were you in the work? Just show me with your fingers."

💡 Assessment Philosophy for Project Days

Project Days assess application and process, not creative output quality. A student can write a mediocre story but demonstrate excellent prompt engineering and editorial judgment—that's what we're assessing.

Focus on: Evidence of systematic approach (iteration, evaluation), metacognitive awareness (reflection on process), engagement with the creative work, documentation of experience

Don't focus on: Story quality or creativity of the final product, writing mechanics or grammar, whether you personally like their creative choices

♿ Differentiation & Support

For Students Struggling to Get Started

Challenge Support Strategy
"I don't know what to work on" • "What part of your story needs development? Characters? Setting? Plot? Pick one."
• "Look at your project outline—what's incomplete?"
• "What happened in the last scene you wrote? What comes next?"
"I don't know what to prompt" • Reference Lesson 3.2 prompt structures
• "Try: 'Write a [story element] that [specific qualities]'"
• "Start simple—just ask for what you need"
Perfectionism/analysis paralysis • "Remember: iteration beats perfection. Start anywhere."
• "Get something on the page, even if it's not perfect. You can improve it."
• "Your editorial eye will tell you what needs fixing"

For Students Who Finish Early

If a student completes meaningful work before time is up:

  • "Excellent progress. Now iterate on what you have—make it even better."
  • "Try an alternative version. Prompt for a different approach to the same story element."
  • "Document in your field journal: what prompt strategies worked best for you today?"
  • "Work on a different story element that needs development."

Extension: "Compare three different AI-generated versions of the same story element. Which works best? Why? Document your editorial reasoning."

For Specific Learning Needs

Need Accommodation
Attention challenges • Break the 35-40 minutes into chunks (20 min work → 2 min break → 15 min work)
• Use fidget tools if helpful
• Allow standing/movement breaks
• Provide work-time checklist
Anxiety about open-ended creative work • Provide specific mini-goals: "Write 2 prompts, evaluate responses, pick one"
• Focus on process, not product: "Just practice the skills"
• Allow partnership with trusted peer for first 10 minutes
Writing/typing challenges • Allow voice-to-text for prompts
• Scribe support if needed
• Focus field journal reflection on verbal processing then transcribe
ESL students • Allow native language prompting if AI supports it
• Focus on story development, not language mechanics
• Pair with supportive partner if desired

🚧 Anticipated Challenges & Solutions

Challenge 1: Students Produce Minimal Work

If many students seem to accomplish little during work time:

Possible causes: Unclear about what to do, stuck on decisions, technical difficulties, avoidance/disengagement

Solutions for next Project Day: Provide clearer opening directions with specific starting points, build in a 5-minute mid-session check-in to troubleshoot, offer more structured prompts or sentence stems, individual conferences to identify blockers

Challenge 2: Students Get Off-Task

In the moment: Quiet redirect: "I notice you're [doing X]. Let's get back to your project." or "What's preventing you from working right now?"

For next time: Clearer expectations about device use, visual cues (timer, progress tracker), more frequent circulation, consider reducing work time length if attention is unsustainable

Challenge 3: Technical Issues with Alia-Creative

Immediate response: Have backup activity ready (work on story without AI, plan next prompts on paper). Allow students to work on character development, plot outlines, or setting descriptions manually. Document the issue for tech support.

Challenge 4: Students Struggle with "Flow" Reflection

Provide sentence stems:

  • "I felt most engaged when..."
  • "Time seemed to pass quickly/slowly when I was..."
  • "Working with AI felt like..."
  • "The hardest part was... The easiest part was..."

Challenge 5: Wide Variance in Progress

This is normal for creative work. Different students work at different paces. Focus assessment on process not output. Tomorrow's discussion can accommodate different experiences. Trust that application happens at individual pace.
